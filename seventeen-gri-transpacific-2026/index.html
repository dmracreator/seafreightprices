Seventeen rate increases in eight months: reading the transpacific GRI cadence

An increase that sticks does not need repeating a fortnight later. The frequency, not the level, is what the transpacific is telling you — and the exposure it creates is about quote validity.

SeaFreightPrices Market Desk · 4 September 2026 · 6 min read

Market data as of 4 September 2026. Rates move weekly. For the current position on your own lanes, use the rate explorer.

Key takeaways

  • The 1 September general rate increase held on both US coasts and pushed transpacific spot rates to fresh peak-season highs.
  • It was the seventeenth increase on the East Asia to USA lane this year, against roughly six in a normal year, and a further one was expected around 15 September.
  • A high increase frequency signals erosion between increases, not sustained strength — carriers are replacing rate that is leaking away rather than building on a stable base.
  • The practical exposure for a forwarder is quote validity: a cargo-ready date that slips past an effective date can reprice a booking that has not otherwise changed.

Seventeen general rate increases in eight months is roughly one every fortnight. On a lane that in a normal year sees about six, that cadence is the story — more so than the level any single increase reached.

What happened on 1 September

CMA CGM, COSCO, Evergreen, Hapag-Lloyd, HMM, Yang Ming and ZIM all filed for the 1 September general rate increase on the East Asia to USA lane. It went through on both coasts, and spot rates set fresh peak-season highs, according to Alba's September 2026 market update.

The supporting data lines up. Drewry assessed Shanghai–Los Angeles up 5% at $7,185 per 40ft on 3 September and Shanghai–New York up 3% at $9,587. Freightos, measuring regional aggregates rather than named port pairs, had Asia–US West Coast up 2% at $7,621 per FEU and the East Coast up 2% at $9,791.

A further increase was expected around 15 September.

Why seventeen

An increase that sticks does not need to be repeated a fortnight later. The frequency itself tells you what is happening between the effective dates: rate is eroding, and carriers are refiling to recover ground rather than to build on it.

Three conditions have made that cycle work this year. Peak-season demand arrived early, in May, and stayed firmer than forecast. Capacity discipline has been sustained — blank sailings have been used steadily rather than reactively, with Drewry counting six announced on the transpacific for the week after 3 September, double the previous week. And congestion at Chinese ports following a run of typhoons since mid-July has tightened effective capacity independently of anything carriers chose to do.

Take any one of those away and a fortnightly increase cadence stops working. That is worth remembering when a customer asks whether rates will keep climbing: the answer depends on three separate conditions holding simultaneously, and one of them is weather.

An increase is a proposal until it is an invoice

A filed GRI is an announcement, not a market outcome. The useful question is never "has an increase been announced" but "did the last one hold, and at what proportion of the filed amount". A pattern of increases holding at half the filed number is a different market from one where they hold in full.

What a GRI actually signals

Unlike a bunker or currency adjustment, a general rate increase has no arithmetic behind it. There is no index reference, no consumption assumption, no reset formula — the distinction that separates formula-driven charges from discretionary ones in our guide to container shipping surcharges. A GRI is a judgement about what the market will bear, tested on a date.

That makes it a useful indicator but a poor forecast. It tells you the balance of supply and demand on the effective date. It tells you nothing about the week after, which is precisely why another one follows so quickly.

It also means the negotiating conversation is different. There is no formula to challenge and no index to check. What you can ask is what triggered it, what proportion is actually being applied to your rate level, and what conditions would see it withdrawn.

Planning around the cadence

The exposure a fortnightly increase cycle creates is not really about the rate level. It is about timing.

  • Pin quote validity in writing. Many spot quotes lapse at the next effective date. If your customer's cargo-ready date is soft, a three-day slip can reprice the booking. Establish the validity date at the point of quoting, not when the invoice is queried.
  • Expect first-of-month and mid-month effective dates. With one expected around 15 September, bookings straddling that date carry repricing risk. Where you can, cut off before it.
  • Separate the rate question from the space question. With blank sailings doubling week on week ahead of Golden Week, an unusable low rate is the more expensive outcome. This is the case for treating allocation as the primary term and rate as secondary.
  • Track whether increases hold, not whether they are filed. Compare the assessed benchmark before and after each effective date on the port pairs you actually quote. Seventeen filings and seventeen successful increases are very different markets, and the rate explorer will show you which one you are in.

What decides the rest of September is the same set of conditions that got the market here: whether Far East congestion eases, how deep the Golden Week withdrawals go, and whether carriers hold capacity discipline through the back half of the traditional peak.

Frequently asked questions

What is a general rate increase (GRI)?

A general rate increase is an across-the-board uplift a carrier files on a trade lane, applied to base freight on a stated effective date. Unlike a bunker or currency adjustment it has no formula behind it: the carrier sets the amount and the market decides whether it holds. An increase that fails is quietly withdrawn or discounted away within days.

How many GRIs have there been on the East Asia to USA lane in 2026?

The 1 September increase was the seventeenth of the year on the East Asia to USA lane, according to Alba's September 2026 market update, against roughly six in a normal year. CMA CGM, COSCO, Evergreen, Hapag-Lloyd, HMM, Yang Ming and ZIM all filed for it, and it held on both US coasts.

Does a GRI holding mean the market is tight?

It means the market was tight on the effective date, which is not the same thing. A GRI holds when carriers have withdrawn enough capacity, or demand is firm enough, that shippers cannot find cheaper space that week. A high frequency of increases usually signals that individual increases are eroding quickly and need replacing, rather than that the market is uniformly strong.

When is the next transpacific rate increase expected?

A further increase was expected around 15 September 2026, following the 1 September one. Mid-month and first-of-month effective dates are the usual pattern on the transpacific, which means a booking that slips across an effective date can reprice even though nothing about the shipment changed.

Can I avoid a GRI by booking earlier?

Sometimes, if your rate is confirmed and the carrier honours it to the agreed validity date. That validity is the thing to negotiate. Many spot quotes are valid only to the next effective date, so a cargo-ready date that moves by three days can move your cost. Ask what the quote is valid until, in writing, rather than assuming it holds until the vessel sails.
